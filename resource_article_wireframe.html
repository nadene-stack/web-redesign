Why most construction schedules fail

Most schedules die in the first two weeks—not because the plan was wrong, but because nobody in the field trusts it enough to keep updating it. A schedule that lives in one person's laptop isn't a schedule. It's a guess with a deadline.

The fix isn't a better tool. It's a schedule built the way the work actually happens: rolling, visible, and owned by the people doing it—not just the people tracking it.

💡
Tip: Build the first draft of the schedule with your superintendent in the room, not for them to review afterward. Buy-in during the build beats buy-in after the fact, every time.

Types of construction schedules

Not every schedule serves the same audience — matching the type to the reader is half the job.

Master schedules

The full-project timeline, built for the owner, the lender, and long-range planning. Rarely changes once approved.

When to use a master schedule

Anytime someone outside the day-to-day build needs to see the whole arc of the project at once.

For publicly funded projects

Milestone dates often tie to funding releases, so the master schedule needs to hold up to scrutiny.

For projects with multiple owner stakeholders

A shared master schedule keeps everyone pointed at the same dates, even when they're not in the field.

Lookahead schedules

The rolling 2–3 week view your crews and subs actually work from — covered next.

Start with a lookahead, not a master plan

A 12-month master schedule is a planning document. A 2–3 week lookahead is a working document—the one your crews actually check before Monday morning. Build the master plan for the owner and the bank. Build the lookahead for the job site.

Get buy-in from your crew

A schedule nobody checks is worse than no schedule—it gives you false confidence that the plan is working. Confirmation matters more than precision. A rough schedule everyone actually looks at beats a precise one that lives in a drawer.

FAQBlock · FAQs

How far ahead should a construction schedule look?

Keep a master schedule for the full project, but run day-to-day off a rolling 2–3 week lookahead. That's the window most crews and subs can actually plan around.

Who should own updating the schedule?

Whoever is closest to the work — usually a super or PM — not someone back in the office updating it from secondhand reports.

What if my crew won't use a digital schedule?

Start with confirmations by text, not a login. Adoption follows ease, not features.
